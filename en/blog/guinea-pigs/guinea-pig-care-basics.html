Basic guinea pig care: hay, food, housing and company

Roddi Health editorial team · updated 16 November 2026 · overview of open sources

In short

The core of a guinea pig’s diet is unlimited hay and grass. On top of that come a few guinea pig pellets, fresh vegetables every day and clean water. Guinea pigs are social and live in pairs or groups. They need a roomy home with a solid floor, hides and space to move.

Hay and grass

Hay should make up most of the diet and be available at all times. PDSA puts its share at 85–90%, PetMD at about 80%. The RSPCA advises giving fresh grass as often as possible, ideally daily. Lawnmower clippings are dangerous and must not be fed.

Pellets

Choose uniform pellets made for guinea pigs, grass-based, rather than muesli-style mixes. PDSA suggests about one tablespoon per adult guinea pig a day; the RSPCA says a portion following the manufacturer’s instructions, fresh each day.

Vegetables and fruit

PDSA suggests about a cup of vegetables per guinea pig a day, 5–6 different kinds, including some rich in vitamin C. Among foods that can be given often, the RSPCA lists bell peppers, cucumber, celery, romaine lettuce and parsley, while broccoli, spinach, kale, carrot and a quarter of an apple are treats for a few times a week. Fruit is given rarely because of its sugar. Introduce new foods gradually.

Don’t give iceberg lettuce, potato, onion, garlic, mushrooms, avocado, rhubarb, chocolate, bread and biscuits, nuts or grains.

Water

Fresh, clean water must always be available. The RSPCA advises checking it twice a day and making sure it doesn’t freeze in winter. Wash the bowl or bottle every day.

Housing

Sources give different minimum sizes:

SourceMinimum
PetMDa cage of about 91 × 76 × 46 cm
PDSAa hutch of at least 1.5 × 1 m and a run of at least 2 × 1 m
RSPCA Australiaat least 2 × 0.5 m for a pair, at least 30 cm high

All agree that bigger is better. The floor should be solid, not mesh. The RSPCA advises having one more hide than there are guinea pigs, and giving them space to move every day. PetMD suggests at least an hour a day outside the cage.

Temperature

The RSPCA gives 17–20 °C as a comfortable room temperature. Above 26 °C the risk of heatstroke rises, and below 15 °C a guinea pig can get chilled. PDSA warns of heatstroke above 27 °C. Keep the home away from radiators, sunny windows and draughts.

Bedding and cleaning

Dust-free hay or paper bedding works well; cotton wool should not be used. Sources disagree about wood shavings, so paper bedding is the safer choice. Remove wet bedding and leftover fresh food every day, and do a thorough clean once a week.

Company

The RSPCA advises keeping a guinea pig with at least one friendly companion unless a vet advises otherwise. Good pairings are a neutered male with one or more females, two females, or brothers raised together. A male living with females must be neutered: according to PDSA, females can become pregnant from about three weeks old. A rabbit is not a companion for a guinea pig, and keeping them together is not advised.

Health checks

  • Check your guinea pig every day: face and eyes, coat, rear end and feet.
  • Check the front teeth and nails once a week. Overgrown teeth must be corrected by a vet.
  • In hot weather check the rear end twice a day: soiled fur attracts flies.
  • Take your guinea pig to a vet at least once a year.
  • If eating, drinking or droppings change, or droppings stop, contact a vet straight away. Difficulty breathing is an emergency.

What to note down before you call the vet

  • how many guinea pigs live together and their sex
  • the size of the home
  • what you feed and how much
  • room temperature
  • weight over time
