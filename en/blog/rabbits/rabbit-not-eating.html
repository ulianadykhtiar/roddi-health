Rabbit not eating: why it’s dangerous and what to do

Roddi Health editorial team · updated 16 November 2026 · overview of open sources

In short

A rabbit needs to eat and pass droppings all the time. PetMD considers more than 8 hours without food or droppings abnormal, and gut stasis an emergency. The Rabbit Welfare Association advises seeing a vet straight away in that case, without waiting until tomorrow.

If your animal’s condition gets worse, see a vet straight away. Don’t put off the visit and don’t treat your pet yourself based on this article.

See a vet urgently if:

  • the rabbit isn’t eating or droppings have stopped
  • teeth grinding, hunched posture
  • a bloated or painful belly
  • cold ears and feet

Why it is so dangerous

A rabbit’s digestion depends on food moving through the gut all the time. When it slows down, the balance of gut bacteria is upset and gas and toxins build up. The RSPCA warns that stasis can quickly become life-threatening. PetMD adds that after more than a day without food a rabbit also risks liver damage. A healthy rabbit, according to the Rabbit Welfare Association, produces up to 300 droppings a day, so fewer droppings are a clear warning sign.

Possible causes

According to Walkerville Vet, the most common cause is gut stasis, but appetite can also go because of:

  • dental problems
  • kidney disease
  • abscesses and pneumonia
  • liver lobe torsion
  • intestinal blockage

Stasis itself, according to PetMD, can be triggered by anything that causes pain, reduces appetite or dehydrates the rabbit, as well as stress and a low-fibre diet. Walkerville stresses that only a vet can find the cause, and that telling stasis from a blockage is especially tricky.

How long can you wait

Sources differ slightly: PetMD considers more than 8 hours without eating or passing droppings abnormal, while Walkerville Vet advises seeing a vet if appetite hasn’t improved within 12 hours or the rabbit is quiet. The RSPCA and the Rabbit Welfare Association advise going straight away. The safest choice is the shortest time.

What to tell the vet

  • When the rabbit last ate and last passed droppings.
  • What you feed and whether the diet changed.
  • Any medicines given recently.
  • How its behaviour has changed.
  • Whether a female rabbit is spayed.

What not to do at home

  • Don’t massage the belly: Walkerville advises ruling out a blockage first.
  • Don’t rely on pineapple, papaya or hairball lubricants. Walkerville says they are usually unnecessary and may even be harmful, and the Rabbit Welfare Association says pineapple won’t help and its sugar may make things worse.
  • Don’t give medicines without a vet’s approval.

How to lower the risk

The RSPCA and the Rabbit Welfare Association recommend a diet of about 85% hay and grass, 10% leafy greens and 5% pellets. Hay must always be available: PetMD warns that rabbits fed only pellets are at higher risk of stasis. Walkerville advises avoiding processed food, fruit, grains, sugar and lucerne hay. The RSPCA also suggests keeping a note of changes in appetite, droppings, behaviour and weight.

What to note down before you call the vet

  • when it last ate
  • when it last passed droppings and how big they were
  • diet and any changes
  • stress, surgery, other illnesses
  • any medicines given recently
