How to make a rat’s life more interesting: company, play and foraging

Roddi Health editorial team · updated 16 November 2026 · overview of open sources

In short

Rats are clever, curious and social, so they need at least one companion and new experiences every day. A tall cage with several levels, hides, ropes, digging boxes and food they have to search for help prevent boredom, and time outside the cage should always be supervised.

Why rats get bored

The RSPCA describes rats as highly intelligent, curious animals with good memories. They learn quickly and need chances to climb, run, forage, explore, play and dig. Without these, a rat gets bored. The RSPCA says chewing the cage bars may signal boredom or a wish to escape, and bald patches from rats chewing each other’s fur may mean they don’t get on or are bored.

Company

A rat is never kept alone: according to the RSPCA, even plenty of human attention doesn’t replace other rats, and loneliness can lead to depression and abnormal behaviour. Keep pairs or small same-sex groups, ideally siblings. In a mixed group, all males must be neutered. Don’t add or remove rats from a stable group. Keep dogs and cats away: to rats they are predators.

A cage with plenty to do

  • The cage should be large enough for the rat to run, stand on its hind legs, climb and explore. PetMD gives a minimum of about 61 × 61 × 61 cm for one rat and advises the largest home possible. RSPCA Australia notes there is no evidence-based size standard.
  • Several levels with ladders and ramps. The RSPCA says levels also reduce squabbles between cage-mates.
  • Bar spacing narrow enough that a rat can’t push its head through: about 1.3 cm according to PetMD, up to 2.5 cm for adults according to RSPCA Australia.
  • A solid floor: wire mesh hurts feet.
  • One more hide than there are rats, with several exits so they can all sleep together.
  • Tunnels, natural-fibre ropes, hammocks, ladders. Remove a fabric hammock once it is chewed so the rat doesn’t swallow the fabric.
  • Change things around: the RSPCA advises rotating toys regularly.

Food they have to find

The RSPCA suggests turning feeding into foraging: mix food into the bedding, scatter and hide it, put it in cardboard tubes or egg boxes. Whole foods that take some work, such as a cooled boiled egg in its shell, are good too. This food should come out of the daily ration, not be added to it. Several feeding spots reduce competition.

Digging and gnawing

For digging, the RSPCA suggests a box of clean sand or compost and a thick layer of bedding in the cage; RSPCA Australia gives 3–4 cm. Rats’ incisors grow throughout life, so they need things to gnaw: wooden chews made for rodents, apple branches, untreated wood.

Time outside the cage

PetMD says rats need time outside the cage every day. The RSPCA advises letting out only tame, relaxed rats, and only under supervision, in a room they can’t escape from and where they can’t get hurt. Remove cables, toxic plants and anything they could chew. An alternative is a large pen with objects, a digging area and a foraging tray. Sources don’t give a length of time.

How to train

Rats are very trainable: the RSPCA mentions teaching them to respond to their name or give a high five. Use rewards only and never punish. A useful skill is climbing onto the scales for a treat, which makes weight checks easier. According to the RSPCA, rats are best handled in the morning or evening: let the rat sniff your hand, slide one palm under its body and rest the other lightly over its back. Never lift a rat by the tail or the scruff.

What to avoid

  • Exercise balls: the RSPCA says they restrict exploration and are stressful.
  • Scented or coloured bedding, cotton wool and fluffy fibres.
  • Strong smells (air fresheners, smoke) and sudden noise, including ultrasound from appliances.
  • Glass and solid plastic cages: air doesn’t circulate well.
  • Trimming whiskers.

When boredom may be illness

The RSPCA advises showing a vet any repetitive behaviour, such as constant bar chewing. Signs of stress also include excessive grooming of the head and paws, aggression, red staining around the eyes and nose, hiding and avoiding contact. Rats hide pain, so any change in behaviour is a reason to look more closely.

What to note down before you call the vet

  • how many rats live together and their sex
  • cage size and number of levels
  • what behaviour changed and since when
  • whether the rat chews the bars or has bald patches
  • weight over time
