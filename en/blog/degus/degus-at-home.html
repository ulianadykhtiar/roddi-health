Degus at home: housing, company, diet and health

Roddi Health editorial team · updated 16 November 2026 · overview of open sources

In short

Degus are active by day and live in groups, so they are not kept alone. They need a tall metal cage with several levels and a wheel, deep bedding for digging and dust baths a few times a week. The core of the diet is hay, with a few sugar-free pellets, because degus are prone to diabetes.

Lifestyle and company

Degus are active by day and very vocal. They are social animals: the RSPCA warns that alone they can become depressed and advises keeping a single degu only on a vet’s advice. Keep single-sex groups or a neutered male with females, with no more than one male in a mixed group. PetMD advises against housing degus with other rodents or rabbits. Sources give different lifespans: PDSA says 6–8 years, sometimes 9; the RSPCA 6–7 years; PetMD 5–9 years.

The cage

  • The cage must be metal: degus chew through plastic and wood. PetMD advises avoiding plastic cages and accessories.
  • Size: PDSA advises as large a cage as possible, and PetMD gives a minimum of about 71 × 46 × 71 cm for two degus, with bars no more than 1.3 cm apart.
  • A tall cage with several levels and solid shelves and floor. A solid roof helps degus feel safe.
  • Pear, apple or beech branches for climbing and gnawing.
  • A nest box with hay for sleeping.
  • Glass tanks are not suitable: the RSPCA links them to breathing problems.
  • Keep the cage indoors, out of draughts and direct sun, away from the TV and music: vibration frightens them.

Heat

The RSPCA advises keeping the room with the cage no warmer than 20 °C and placing it away from radiators. A degu lying flat on its stomach may be overheating: contact a vet straight away.

Exercise and digging

A solid wheel is needed, big enough for the degu to run with a straight back. PetMD gives a diameter of 25–30 cm. PDSA advises letting degus out for at least half an hour a day into a safe room or pen with wires and wood covered. For digging they need a deep layer of hay and shredded paper. Sources disagree on wood shavings: the RSPCA allows non-toxic shavings, while PetMD advises against them because of the risk to breathing.

Diet

  • The core is unlimited hay.
  • Degu pellets: PDSA and PetMD suggest one to two tablespoons a day. If degu pellets are not available, guinea pig or chinchilla pellets without molasses will do.
  • The RSPCA considers rabbit, hamster and gerbil food, and mixes with dried fruit, unsuitable.
  • Leafy greens and vegetables in small portions: PDSA suggests once or twice a week, PetMD a few times a week.
  • PetMD advises no fruit, honey, molasses or seeds at all. PDSA allows an occasional bit of apple. The RSPCA allows carrot, sweet potato and beetroot only in tiny pieces now and then.
  • Be careful with shop-bought treats: they are often too sugary.
  • Degus eat some of their own droppings: this is normal.
  • Introduce new food gradually.

Diabetes

According to the RSPCA and PDSA, degus are prone to type 2 diabetes, especially on a diet high in carbohydrates and sugar. PDSA lists these signs: cloudy eyes (cataracts), drinking a lot and urinating often, and weight gain. If you notice any of these, see a vet.

Teeth

Degus’ teeth grow continuously and wear down as they chew hay. The RSPCA says healthy teeth are yellow-orange, white teeth can be a sign of illness, and advises checking them every week. According to PDSA, dental problems can show as drooling, a swollen face, discharge from the eyes and a change in tooth colour. Only a vet should correct overgrown teeth.

The tail and handling

Never pick up or pull a degu by the tail. The skin on the tip of the tail can come off, and then a vet is needed because of the risk of infection. The RSPCA advises letting the degu climb onto your palm and lifting it in cupped hands, low over the floor. Hands swooping down from above frighten them.

Health checks

The RSPCA advises checking degus every day. A healthy degu has bright, clear eyes, clean ears, a glossy coat, and a clean tail and rear end. Warning signs include nasal discharge, wetness around the mouth, cloudy eyes and white teeth. Take your degus to a vet at least once a year. PetMD advises seeing a vet urgently if a degu eats less, loses weight, breathes with difficulty, has diarrhoea, struggles to chew or becomes lethargic.

What to note down before you call the vet

  • how many degus live together and their sex
  • what you feed and whether they get sweet things
  • how much the degus drink
  • the condition of the eyes and tooth colour
  • room temperature
