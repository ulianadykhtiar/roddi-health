Chinchilla health: teeth, fur, digestion and warning signs

Roddi Health editorial team · updated 16 November 2026 · overview of open sources

In short

A chinchilla’s teeth grow throughout life, so the core of its diet is unlimited hay, not sugary mixes. Saliva on the chin, a poorer appetite, weight loss or fewer droppings can mean mouth pain or digestive trouble. Chinchillas hide illness, so these signs are a reason to see a vet straight away.

If your animal’s condition gets worse, see a vet straight away. Don’t put off the visit and don’t treat your pet yourself based on this article.

See a vet urgently if:

  • the chinchilla is not eating, or there are fewer or no droppings
  • drooling, a wet chin
  • lethargy, moving less
  • difficulty breathing
  • diarrhoea or soft droppings

Why spotting problems early matters

PetMD notes that chinchillas are good at hiding illness, so dental problems can go unnoticed until they are severe. PDSA and the RSPCA recommend checking your chinchilla every day, and the RSPCA also advises a vet check-up at least once a year.

Teeth

A chinchilla’s teeth grow continuously and wear down through chewing. That is why the core of the diet is hay and grass, and PetMD advises unlimited good-quality hay. Pellets are given sparingly, without sugary treats. PDSA advises avoiding muesli-style mixes: chinchillas pick out the sugary bits, which can lead to painful dental disease. Chew blocks or untreated apple or pear branches are good for gnawing.

Signs of dental problems, according to PetMD:

  • drooling and wet fur on the chin
  • eating less, dropping food, pawing at the mouth
  • weight loss and fewer droppings
  • swelling of the jaw or face
  • overgrown or crooked front teeth
  • less grooming and lethargy

In a 2001 UK study (Crossley), dental changes were found on external examination in 35% of chinchillas that looked healthy. The author also noted that jaw swelling, weight loss or changes in chewing occur with various dental problems, so a vet has to find the actual cause. According to PetMD, examining the back teeth usually needs sedation and X-rays, and teeth must never be trimmed at home. If pain stops a chinchilla eating, gut stasis or infection can develop and become life-threatening within hours or days.

Digestion

The RSPCA advises checking every day that your chinchilla eats and leaves plenty of dry droppings. If eating or drinking habits change, or droppings decrease or stop, contact a vet straight away: the chinchilla may be seriously ill. Diarrhoea and soft droppings are also a reason for a visit.

Fur and skin

Fur loss can be a sign of illness or injury. The RSPCA also lists chewing its own or others’ fur, and patches of fur coming away when the chinchilla is handled, as signs of stress. Fur release is a defence response that can happen with rough handling. Without dust baths the fur becomes matted; we cover them in a separate article.

Heat and humidity

Dense fur is an adaptation to cold, so heat and humidity are dangerous for chinchillas. Sources give slightly different ranges: the RSPCA recommends 10–18 °C in a dry, draught-free room, while PetMD gives roughly 13–21 °C, never above 27 °C, with humidity below 40–50% and no direct sun.

Daily check

  • Eyes and nose free of discharge, fur around the eyes and mouth dry.
  • The chinchilla eats and drinks, and droppings are normal and plentiful.
  • No bald patches, no skin changes.
  • No sore or injured feet, no limping.
  • No unusual swellings.
  • Stable weight: PetMD advises monitoring body weight.

What to note down before you call the vet

  • when the chinchilla last ate normally
  • how many droppings a day and what they look like
  • weight over time
  • photos of the chin, teeth and fur
  • what you feed: hay, pellets, treats
  • room temperature
