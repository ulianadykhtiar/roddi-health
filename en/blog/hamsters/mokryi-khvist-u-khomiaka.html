Wet tail in hamsters: symptoms and what to do

Roddi Health editorial team · updated 16 November 2026 · overview of open sources

In short

“Wet tail” is diarrhoea that soaks the fur under the tail. A hamster loses fluid quickly, so it is often an emergency: PetMD advises that a vet who treats exotic animals should see the hamster, ideally within 24 hours.

If your animal’s condition gets worse, see a vet straight away. Don’t put off the visit and don’t treat your pet yourself based on this article.

See a vet urgently if:

  • wet, matted fur under the tail and on the belly
  • watery or soft droppings
  • the hamster isn’t eating, is lethargic, hides
  • a bloated or painful belly, hunched posture

What it is

The term usually refers to diarrhoea in young hamsters, although it can happen at any age. Fluid loss leaves the hamster dehydrated and weak, it eats less and its coat looks scruffy. PetMD warns that without prompt treatment the hamster can die, and PDSA that death is sometimes sudden.

Why it happens

According to PetMD, causes can be infectious: bacteria, especially in hamsters 3–10 weeks old, protozoa and intestinal parasites. They can also be non-infectious:

  • a sudden change of food or brand
  • too many pellets, vegetables, fruit or sweet treats
  • stress: transport, heat or high humidity, overcrowding
  • a dirty cage and spoiled food in hidden stores
  • some antibiotics (PetMD names penicillin) that kill the good bacteria in the gut

What to do now

  • Call a vet who treats exotic animals as soon as possible.
  • Don’t give medicines, especially antibiotics, without a prescription: the RSPCA warns that some antibiotics can cause serious digestive problems in hamsters.
  • Take a fresh droppings sample and note what you feed, the room temperature and humidity, and whether any new hamsters arrived.
  • Handle the hamster less and don’t disturb it more than necessary.
  • Some bacteria that cause wet tail can infect people. Wash your hands after contact and ask the vet how to handle the hamster during treatment.

The vet may give fluids, medicines and supportive care. According to PetMD, improvement is often seen within two to three days, and recovery takes one to two weeks or longer. Don’t change the diet during recovery unless the vet advises it.

How to lower the risk

  • A calm home without temperature swings.
  • Remove leftover food and dirty bedding every day.
  • A balanced diet without sudden changes; store food in an airtight tub or jar.
  • Don’t overfeed vegetables and fruit.
  • Keep new hamsters apart for at least 30 days, as PetMD advises.

What to note down before you call the vet

  • when the symptoms appeared
  • the hamster’s age
  • changes in food, new animals, stress
  • any medicines given recently
  • when it last ate and drank
  • room temperature and humidity
